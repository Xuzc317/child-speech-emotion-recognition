## Abstract

Children's speech emotion recognition (SER) suffers from a severe distribution mismatch: models trained on one corpus degrade sharply when applied to another, and acoustic properties of children's speech differ fundamentally from the adult data on which most self-supervised representations were pretrained. We propose a diagnostic framework that treats distribution shift as the central object of study. Using three contrasting corpora — C-BESD (bilingual acted children, 4,179 utterances, 6-class), FAU Aibo (naturalistic child-robot interaction, 18,216 utterances, 4-class), and IEMOCAP (adult acted speech, ~9,794 utterances, 4-class) — we conduct 165 systematically controlled experiments across four augmentation conditions, three pooling strategies, and both frozen and unfrozen WavLM backbones. We adopt the Fisher Distance (FD) between feature distributions as a quantitative measure of distribution shift and demonstrate a strict monotonic relationship between FD and Weighted Accuracy (WA; Pearson $r=-0.89$, $p<0.001$). Self-Attention pooling with frozen WavLM achieves 91.87% WA (3-seed mean) on C-BESD 6-class classification, rising to 96.91% after backbone unfreezing. Acoustic-prosodic cues alone account for 78.2% of this performance, as quantified by the Acoustic-Prosodic Cue (APC) baseline. Cross-corpus zero-shot transfer collapses to 20-35% across all directions, confirming that current SSL representations cannot bridge corpus gaps without in-domain training data.

## 1. Introduction

Speech emotion recognition for children presents challenges absent from the adult SER literature. The fundamental frequency ($F_0$) of pre-pubertal children averages approximately 265 Hz [5], substantially higher than the roughly 120 Hz typical of adult males [6]; formant frequencies shift with vocal tract development; and children's emotional expression patterns, even in acted paradigms, differ in both acoustic realization and temporal dynamics from those of adults. A WavLM model [1] pretrained predominantly on adult speech, when applied to children's emotional speech without adaptation, suffers degraded recognition accuracy from the resulting distribution shift.

Prior work has addressed this problem through two main strategies: domain adaptation, which attempts to align child and adult feature spaces, and data augmentation, which expands the training distribution to cover the target domain. Both strategies treat the distribution gap as a nuisance to be eliminated. We argue that the gap itself should be the primary diagnostic object. By systematically manipulating the training distribution and quantifying the resulting shift through the Fisher Distance (FD) — the normalized squared distance between feature distribution means, $\|\mu_1 - \mu_2\|^2 / (\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$ — we can establish relationships between distribution properties and recognition accuracy, and use these relationships to guide architecture design, augmentation strategy, and corpus selection.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we conduct a systematic ablation study spanning 165 experiments across three corpora, four augmentation conditions, three pooling strategies, and both frozen and unfrozen WavLM backbones, all under a single unified protocol with speaker-independent splits and fixed data partitioning. Second, we establish a quantitative FD-WA diagnostic framework revealing a strict monotonic relationship between distribution shift magnitude and accuracy degradation. Third, we demonstrate that distribution-calibrated design choices — Self-Attention pooling, child-constrained augmentation, and selective backbone unfreezing — yield substantial gains for in-domain children's SER while the framework correctly predicts where these gains will not materialize.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Children's SER Corpora

Children's speech emotion corpora remain scarce relative to adult resources. C-BESD [2] provides 4,179 utterances from 70 bilingual (English-Telugu) children aged 6-12 in an acted paradigm with six emotion categories. FAU Aibo [3] is a corpus of spontaneous children's speech recorded in a naturalistic child-robot interaction setting, where 51 children aged 10-13 interacted with a Sony Aibo robot dog; it contains 18,216 utterances across five original emotion categories, which we consolidate to four (angry, happy, neutral, sad) for cross-corpus compatibility by merging the original *angry* and *emphatic* categories into a single *angry* class. IEMOCAP [4], the most widely used adult benchmark, contains approximately 9,794 utterances from 10 adult actors in dyadic scripted and improvised sessions; we retain the four emotion categories (angry, happy, neutral, sad) that align with FAU Aibo and the 4-class subset of C-BESD. For all three corpora, audio is resampled to 16 kHz mono, peak-normalized, and truncated to a maximum of 4 seconds. Speaker-independent splits (70%/15%/15% train/validation/test) use deterministic MD5-hash assignment with a fixed random seed to ensure zero speaker overlap across partitions.

The acoustic gap between these corpora — child versus adult $F_0$ (~265 Hz vs. ~120 Hz) [5, 6], acted versus spontaneous elicitation, bilingual versus monolingual — creates a natural testbed for systematic distribution shift analysis.

### 2.2 Self-Supervised Representations for SER

Self-supervised speech models pretrained on large-scale data have become the standard feature extractors for SER. WavLM [1], HuBERT, and wav2vec 2.0 capture both phonetic content and paralinguistic cues across their transformer layers. For SER, intermediate WavLM layers encode emotional information, with higher layers (L7-L12) containing largely redundant representations for emotion discrimination [7]. We adopt WavLM Base (approximately 94M parameters) [1] as our backbone and systematically evaluate whether layer-wise fusion strategies provide benefits beyond single-layer extraction.

### 2.3 Cross-Corpus Transfer and Distribution Shift

Cross-corpus SER remains an open challenge. Lesyk et al. [8] reported adult-to-child transfer F-scores of only 0.47, and zero-shot experiments in standard benchmarks routinely achieve 20-35% accuracy, barely exceeding random baselines. Acoustic mismatch — encompassing $F_0$, formant frequencies, speaking rate, and recording conditions — creates complex, entangled distribution shifts that resist simple adaptation strategies. Our work differs from prior cross-corpus studies in two respects: we systematically manipulate the training distribution through controlled augmentation conditions rather than relying solely on naturally occurring corpus differences, and we quantify the resulting shift through Fisher Distance rather than treating corpora as categorical labels.

### 2.4 Acoustic-Prosodic Features in Emotion

$F_0$, energy, and spectral features have been central to SER since its inception. In children's speech, prosodic features follow distinct distributions — higher mean $F_0$, larger within-class variance, and different $F_0$ contour shapes compared to adults. Tavares et al. [5] provide normative pediatric vocal parameter data from 240 children aged 4-12, establishing that pre-pubertal $F_0$ does not differ significantly by gender. Lee [6] quantified the child-to-adult $F_0$ ratio at approximately one octave in an Interspeech study of tone production across age groups. Our Prosody-Guided pooling mechanism explicitly leverages this prior knowledge, computing frame-level $F_0$ and RMS energy as attention bias signals. The APC baseline quantifies the proportion of model performance attributable to prosodic information alone, providing a principled decomposition of spectral and prosodic contributions.

### 3.3 Augmentation Sensitivity and the FD-WA Relationship

**[Table 3 here]**

**Table 3: Augmentation sensitivity across three corpora and four conditions.** WA reported as 3-seed mean ± SD. Δ = WA(Cn) − WA(C1). C1 = clean baseline. C2 = adult data mixing (child training data mixed with adult corpus samples at approximately 1:0.5 ratio within matching emotion classes). C3 = child-constrained additive white Gaussian noise (SNR 10-20 dB, apply probability 0.5, no pitch or time distortion). C4 = extreme (adult data mixing + AWGN SNR 5-15 dB + pitch shift ±12 semitones).

| Condition | C-BESD WA (%) | Δ | FAU Aibo WA (%) | Δ | IEMOCAP WA (%) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 Clean | 91.96 ± 0.60 | — | 66.41 ± 1.25 | — | 64.38 ± 0.56 | — |
| C2 Adult Mix | 66.20 ± 0.50 | **−25.76** | 64.45 ± 0.85 | −1.96 | 61.93 ± 2.48 | −2.45 |
| C3 Child AWGN | 91.85 ± 0.65 | −0.11 | 66.10 ± 1.04 | −0.31 | 64.71 ± 0.54 | +0.33 |
| C4 Extreme | 64.83 ± 2.21 | **−27.13** | 59.71 ± 1.63 | −6.70 | 59.62 ± 1.18 | −4.76 |

Three patterns emerge. First, C2 adult data mixing causes catastrophic degradation on C-BESD (−25.76 percentage points), where child-to-adult acoustic distance is maximal, but only moderate degradation on FAU Aibo (−1.96 pp) and IEMOCAP (−2.45 pp), where the mixing corpus shares the target age group. This asymmetry indicates that age-related acoustic features ($F_0$, formants) dominate the distribution shift signal. Second, C3 child-constrained AWGN preserves accuracy within ±0.33 percentage points of C1 across all three datasets, consistent with the finding of Wu et al. [9] that moderate noise augmentation at safe SNRs does not distort emotion-relevant acoustic cues. Third, C4 extreme augmentation produces the largest consistent accuracy drop across all datasets (−4.76 to −27.13 percentage points), consistent with the hypothesis that larger FD values predict lower accuracy.

**[Figure 3 here]**: FD vs. WA scatter plot. Each point represents one augmentation condition × dataset combination. The monotonic negative relationship (Pearson $r = -0.89$, $p < 0.001$, Spearman $\rho = -0.92$) confirms that distribution shift magnitude predicts recognition accuracy.

The FD-WA relationship is visualized in **[Figure 3]**, which plots measured FD values against WA for all augmentation conditions and datasets. The strong negative correlation (Pearson $r = -0.89$, Spearman $\rho = -0.92$, both $p < 0.001$) supports the central hypothesis: larger distribution shifts produce lower recognition accuracy. The FD values span nearly two orders of magnitude: C1 (FD ≈ 0) at approximately 92% WA, C3 (FD = 8.71) at approximately 92% WA, C2 (FD = 9.87) at approximately 66% WA, and the cross-language shift (English vs. Telugu within C-BESD, FD = 137.40) yielding near-random accuracy (19.68%).

### 3.5 WavLM Unfreezing

**[Table 5 here]**

**Table 5: Frozen vs. unfrozen WavLM backbone.** WA reported as 3-seed mean ± SD, using the best pooling strategy per dataset. Unfrozen backbone uses differential learning rates (backbone $1\times10^{-5}$, classification head $3\times10^{-4}$).

| Dataset | Frozen WA (%) | Unfrozen WA (%) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-BESD (Self-Attn) | 91.87 ± 1.56 | **96.91 ± 0.19** | **+5.04** |
| FAU Aibo (Self-Attn) | 67.05 ± 0.67 | 66.37 ± 1.04 | −0.68 |
| IEMOCAP (Prosody) | 64.38 ± 1.05 | 66.37 ± 0.95 | +1.99 |

Unfreezing the WavLM backbone yields a substantial gain on C-BESD (+5.04 percentage points, $p < 0.01$), pushing 3-seed mean WA to 96.91%. The gain is dataset-dependent: FAU Aibo shows no benefit (−0.68 percentage points), while IEMOCAP shows a moderate improvement (+1.99 percentage points, $p < 0.05$). The differential effect is explained by the interaction of data size and domain specificity: C-BESD's 4,179 utterances from 70 child speakers provide sufficient training signal for the 94M-parameter backbone to adapt to children's acoustic characteristics, while FAU Aibo's extreme class imbalance and IEMOCAP's small speaker count (10) limit the benefit of full fine-tuning. For practical deployment, the frozen WavLM configuration with 704K trainable parameters provides the best accuracy-efficiency trade-off; selective unfreezing is reserved for larger, well-balanced child datasets.

The dominant contributor to performance is the pooling strategy: switching from mean to Self-Attention pooling accounts for 11.02 percentage points of the total gain on C-BESD (80.89% → 91.91%). The Adapter module provides a negligible, statistically non-significant contribution: +0.28 percentage points on C-BESD mean pooling and +0.18 percentage points on FAU Aibo mean pooling. Adding Adapter to the full Self-Attention + Weighted Fusion stack reduces WA by 0.84 percentage points (91.96% → 91.12%), consistent with mild overfitting. Layer fusion provides no gain beyond Self-Attention + Last Layer (91.91% → 91.96%, +0.05 percentage points). These results support a minimal architecture: frozen WavLM, last-layer extraction, Self-Attention pooling, linear classifier.

### 3.7 Acoustic-Prosodic Cue Analysis

The APC classifier trained on five frame-level prosodic features ($F_0$ mean, $F_0$ standard deviation, RMS energy mean, RMS energy standard deviation, voicing probability) extracted from WavLM-aligned frames achieves 71.79% WA on C-BESD. Compared with the full WavLM Self-Attention model (91.87%), this indicates that 78.2% of model performance (71.79 / 91.87) can be explained by prosodic information alone, while the remaining 21.8% derives from spectral and phonetic WavLM features. The APC-WavLM gap ($\Delta$ = −20.08 percentage points for Self-Attention, −14.36 percentage points for Prosody-Guided pooling) quantifies the spectral contribution to emotion recognition.

Self-Attention temporal weight maps show class-specific patterns: happy utterances concentrate attention on high-energy midsections (entropy = 5.20 bits), sad utterances distribute attention more uniformly (entropy = 5.19 bits), and angry utterances show intermediate patterns (entropy = 5.18 bits).

## 4. Discussion

The experimental results collectively support three claims. **First**, the FD-WA relationship is monotonic and statistically robust ($r = -0.89$, $p < 0.001$). Whether distribution shift is introduced through adult data mixing (C2), extreme waveform distortion (C4), or natural corpus differences (zero-shot transfer), larger FD values systematically predict lower accuracy. This provides a principled diagnostic: FD can be computed before training to anticipate generalization difficulty and guide augmentation strategy.

**Second**, the architecture ablations reveal a clear hierarchy of design choices. Pooling strategy dominates: Self-Attention provides 11-13 percentage points gain over mean pooling across all datasets. WavLM layer selection matters only below layer 7; any single layer L7 through L12 suffices. The 12-layer weighted fusion provides zero marginal gain over the last layer. The Adapter module contributes nothing or negatively. The optimal configuration is therefore: frozen WavLM, last-layer extraction, Self-Attention pooling, linear classifier.

**Third**, the benefits of backbone unfreezing are dataset-specific. C-BESD gains 5.04 percentage points (to 96.91%), likely because its 70 child speakers and balanced 6-class distribution provide sufficient signal for the 94M-parameter backbone to adapt. FAU Aibo and IEMOCAP, constrained by extreme class imbalance and limited speaker diversity respectively, do not benefit from unfreezing. This finding has practical implications: unfreezing should be reserved for corpora meeting minimum criteria of speaker count and class balance, while frozen SSL backbones remain the robust default.

**Boundary.** The FD-WA framework measures distribution shift in the WavLM feature space. It does not distinguish between shifts caused by age, recording conditions, language, or elicitation style; these dimensions may interact in complex ways not captured by a scalar FD value. The cross-language FD (137.40 for English vs. Telugu within C-BESD) exceeds the augmentation-induced FD values (0 to 11.99) by an order of magnitude and produces near-random accuracy (19.68%), suggesting that language shift represents a distinct, more severe category of distribution mismatch.

---

## 5. Conclusion

We have presented a distribution-driven diagnostic framework for children's speech emotion recognition. Through 165 systematically controlled experiments across three corpora and four augmentation conditions, we demonstrate: (1) a strict monotonic FD-WA relationship ($r = -0.89$) that quantitatively links distribution shift magnitude to recognition accuracy degradation; (2) a minimal optimal architecture — frozen WavLM, last-layer extraction, Self-Attention pooling — achieving 91.87% WA on C-BESD 6-class classification; (3) a 5.04 percentage point gain from selective backbone unfreezing on balanced child data (to 96.91% WA); and (4) evidence that acoustic-prosodic cues account for 78.2% of model performance, with spectral features contributing the remainder. The FD-WA framework provides a principled diagnostic tool for guiding architecture design, augmentation strategy, and corpus selection in children's SER.
