# Distribution Shift, Not Architecture: A Systematic Diagnosis of Children's Speech Emotion Recognition Across Corpora

## Abstract

Children's speech emotion recognition (SER) lags behind adult SER despite sharing model architectures and training paradigms. Whether this gap stems from distribution shift between children's speech corpora or from suboptimal architectural design has remained an open question, as prior work has not systematically isolated these factors. Here we present a controlled 192-experiment ablation study using WavLM Base across three corpora—C-BESD (children's acted), FAU Aibo (children's spontaneous), and IEMOCAP (adult acted)—to disentangle the effects of distribution shift from pooling strategy, layer fusion, adapter modules, data augmentation, and transfer learning. We find that distribution shift between corpora, not architecture, is the dominant performance bottleneck: the gap between in-domain children's acted speech (92.92%) and spontaneous speech (67.81%) persists across all architectural configurations. Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer collapses to 19–35%, and transfer learning success depends primarily on the target domain rather than the source. Architectural factors—self-attention pooling provides modest gains (+13.4pp over mean pooling on acted speech), while adapter modules and layer fusion contribute negligibly. These findings indicate that future progress in children's SER requires addressing the acted-spontaneous distribution gap rather than pursuing incremental architectural refinements. All 192 experiment logs, model checkpoints, and evaluation code are publicly released.

## 1. Introduction

Speech emotion recognition (SER) aims to identify emotional states from acoustic signals. While substantial progress has been made on adult speech corpora using self-supervised learning (SSL) models, children's speech emotion recognition remains underexplored despite its importance for educational technology, clinical screening, and child-robot interaction.

Children's speech differs from adult speech along multiple acoustic dimensions—higher and more variable fundamental frequency (F0), shorter utterance durations, and ongoing anatomical development of the vocal tract. These differences raise a fundamental question: is the performance gap between children's and adult SER driven by model architecture choices, or by inherent distributional properties of the speech signal itself?

Prior work has approached children's SER through two main routes: applying SSL models pre-trained on adult speech directly to children's data, and designing specialized pooling or adaptation modules. However, systematic diagnosis of *why* performance varies across children's corpora has been lacking. In particular, the contribution of distribution shift—the statistical mismatch between training and testing distributions—has not been isolated from architectural factors in a controlled experimental framework.

In this work, we present a systematic 192-experiment ablation study that disentangles the effects of distribution shift from architectural design choices in children's SER. Using WavLM Base as a unified backbone across three corpora—C-BESD (children's acted speech), FAU Aibo (children's spontaneous speech), and IEMOCAP (adult acted speech)—we evaluate pooling strategies, layer fusion, adapter modules, data augmentation, and transfer learning under both frozen and unfrozen conditions.

Our key findings are: (1) distribution shift between corpora, not architecture, is the dominant bottleneck—zero-shot cross-corpus performance collapses to 19–35% even with optimal pooling; (2) the acted-versus-spontaneous distinction within children's speech creates a larger performance gap (25pp) than the child-versus-adult distinction; (3) transfer learning success is determined primarily by the target domain, with C-BESD as a target achieving 91%+ regardless of source; (4) adapter modules provide no benefit and can be safely removed; and (5) layer fusion contributes negligibly, as any single transformer layer L≥7 performs equivalently to learned weighted fusion.

We release all 192 experiment logs, model checkpoints, and evaluation code to facilitate reproducible research on children's SER.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Self-Supervised Learning for SER

Self-supervised pre-trained models—including WavLM, HuBERT, wav2vec 2.0, and emotion2vec—have become the standard backbone for speech emotion recognition. WavLM, in particular, achieves strong performance on the SUPERB benchmark through its masked speech denoising pre-training objective. Prior work has shown that frozen SSL features followed by a lightweight classifier can match or exceed fine-tuned models on adult SER benchmarks. Our work extends this paradigm to children's speech and systematically ablates the design choices around these frozen backbones.

### 2.2 Children's Speech Processing

Children's speech presents unique challenges: higher and more variable F0, shorter vocal tract length, ongoing anatomical development, and greater within-speaker variability compared to adults. Studies on children's ASR have demonstrated that models trained on adult speech degrade significantly on children's speech. For SER specifically, the limited availability of labeled children's emotional speech corpora has constrained systematic investigation.

### 2.3 Distribution Shift in Speech

Distribution shift—the mismatch between training and test distributions—is a well-studied problem in machine learning. In speech processing, domain shift arises from differences in recording conditions, speaker demographics, language, and speaking style. Fréchet Distance (FD) has been used to quantify representation-level shift between speech corpora. Our work contributes a systematic diagnosis of how distribution shift specifically affects children's SER, using controlled ablation to separate shift effects from architectural factors.

### 2.4 Pooling and Adaptation for SER

Temporal pooling strategies—from simple mean pooling to learned attention and prosody-guided methods—have been extensively explored for aggregating frame-level SSL features. Adapter modules, originally developed for parameter-efficient transfer learning in NLP, have been applied to SER with mixed results. Our findings that self-attention pooling provides the largest benefit while adapter modules are dispensable contribute evidence to this design discussion.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Corpora

| Corpus | Utterances | Speakers | Classes | Age | Style |
|--------|-----------|----------|---------|-----|-------|
| C-BESD (MY) | 4,179 | 70 children | 6 | 6–12y | Acted |
| FAU Aibo | 18,216 | 51 children | 4 | 10–13y | Spontaneous |
| IEMOCAP | ~9,794 | 10 adults | 4 | — | Acted |

For cross-corpus compatibility, corpora are mapped to four shared emotion classes (angry, happy, neutral, sad) except for C-BESD in-domain evaluations where six native classes are used.

### 3.2 Speaker-Independent Split

Deterministic MD5-hash-based speaker split: 70/15/15 (train/val/test), fixed seed 42. Zero speaker leakage between splits.

### 3.3 Model Architecture

```
WavLM Base (frozen/unfrozen) → 12-layer LayerFusion → Pooling → SEMLP Classifier
```

| Component | Specification | Parameters |
|-----------|--------------|------------|
| SSL Backbone | WavLM Base (wavlm-base-sv) | 94M (frozen) |
| Layer Fusion | 12 learnable weights (softmax) | 12 |
| Pooling | Mean / Self-Attn / Prosody Guided | ~111K |
| Classifier | SEMLP (SE + MLP) | ~593K |
| **Total trainable** | | **~704K** |

### 3.4 Training Protocol

- End-to-end training on raw 16kHz waveforms
- Batch size 16, AdamW optimizer
- 100 epochs max, early stopping (patience=15 on validation WA)
- 3 random seeds (42, 123, 456) per experiment
- Online feature extraction (no pre-computed features)

### 3.5 Experimental Design (B1–B7, 192 runs)

| Phase | Content | Runs |
|-------|---------|------|
| B1 | Pooling × Corpus baseline (frozen) | 27 |
| B2 | Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer | 18 |
| B3 | Data augmentation sensitivity (C1–C4) | 36 |
| B4 | LayerFusion ablation | 54 |
| B5 | WavLM unfreeze comparison | 9 |
| B6 | Module ablation (Adapter/Pooling/Fusion) | 30 |
| B7 | Transfer fine-tuning (6 directions) | 18 |

## 4. Experiments and Results

### 4.1 Baseline: Pooling Strategy Across Corpora (B1)

**Table 1: Pooling baselines (frozen WavLM, 3 seeds)**

| Corpus | Pooling | WA (mean±std) |
|--------|---------|---------------|
| C-BESD | Self-Attention | **92.92±2.15%** |
| C-BESD | Prosody | 87.38±0.79% |
| C-BESD | Mean | 79.56±1.14% |
| FAU Aibo | Self-Attention | **67.81±2.15%** |
| FAU Aibo | Prosody | 65.38±1.23% |
| FAU Aibo | Mean | 64.40±0.76% |
| IEMOCAP | Self-Attention | **65.16±1.34%** |
| IEMOCAP | Mean | 58.86±1.66% |
| IEMOCAP | Prosody | 56.22±0.66% |

Self-attention pooling consistently outperforms alternatives. The 25pp gap between C-BESD (92.92%) and FAU Aibo (67.81%)—both children's corpora—cannot be explained by pooling strategy alone.

### 4.2 Zero-Shot Transfer Reveals Distribution Shift (B2)

**Table 2: Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer**

| Direction | Best WA |
|-----------|---------|
| IEMOCAP → C-BESD | 34.68% |
| FAU → IEMOCAP | 35.47% |
| C-BESD → IEMOCAP | 33.47% |
| C-BESD → FAU | 19.17% |

Zero-shot performance collapses across all directions. The C-BESD→FAU direction is most degraded (19.17%), confirming that acted-to-spontaneous transfer is particularly challenging.

### 4.3 Data Augmentation (B3)

On FAU Aibo: child-domain augmentation (C3) yields marginal gain (+0.57pp); adult-domain (C2) and mixed-domain (C4) cause significant degradation (−6.8 to −9.2pp). Augmentation is only beneficial when the injected distribution matches the target domain.

### 4.4 Layer Fusion (B4)

54 experiments across 3 corpora. All fusion variants within 2pp band. Single layers L≥7 perform equivalently to weighted fusion. **Layer fusion strategy is not a meaningful design choice.**

### 4.5 Unfreezing WavLM (B5)

| Corpus | Frozen WA | Unfrozen WA | Δ |
|--------|-----------|-------------|---|
| C-BESD | 92.92% | **96.91±0.53%** | +4.0pp |
| FAU Aibo | 67.81% | **76.02±0.68%** | +8.2pp |
| IEMOCAP | 65.16% | **66.36±3.30%** | +1.2pp |

Larger gains on corpora with lower frozen baselines—consistent with a ceiling effect.

### 4.6 Module Ablation (B6)

**Table 3: Module ablation on C-BESD and FAU Aibo**

| Configuration | C-BESD WA | FAU WA |
|--------------|-----------|--------|
| Baseline (Mean + last layer) | 80.89% | 67.97% |
| + Adapter | 81.17% | 68.15% |
| + SelfAttn Pooling | **92.24%** | 67.80% |
| + LayerFusion | 91.96% | 66.41% |
| Full stack | 91.12% | 66.11% |

Three findings: (1) Pooling upgrade (Mean→SelfAttn) is the largest contributor on C-BESD (+11.4pp) but negligible on FAU; (2) Adapter is neutral to harmful in all configurations; (3) LayerFusion contributes negligibly. The C-BESD ceiling (~92%) and FAU ceiling (~68%) are inherent to the corpora.

### 4.7 Transfer Learning (B7)

**Table 4: Transfer fine-tuning results**

| Source → Target | WA (mean±std) |
|----------------|---------------|
| FAU → C-BESD | **91.57±0.36%** |
| IEMOCAP → C-BESD | **91.17±1.04%** |
| C-BESD → FAU | 66.82±0.69% |
| IEMOCAP → FAU | 65.97±0.52% |
| C-BESD → IEMOCAP | 63.25±0.40% |
| FAU → IEMOCAP | 62.81±0.85% |

Striking asymmetry: transfers to C-BESD achieve 91%+ regardless of source; transfers to FAU or IEMOCAP stall at 62–67%. **Target domain, not source domain, determines transfer success.**

### 4.8 Confusion Matrix Analysis

Per-class accuracy patterns are corpus-dependent:
- **C-BESD**: all 6 classes >87%, *sad* and *fear* reach 97–100%
- **FAU Aibo**: *neutral* 84–86%, *sad* drops to 1–13%, *happy* to 16–34%
- **IEMOCAP**: *angry* 78–81%, *neutral* hardest at 28–29%

## 5. Analysis and Discussion

### 5.1 Distribution Shift Is the Dominant Bottleneck

The 25pp gap between C-BESD and FAU Aibo under identical architecture confirms that corpus-level distribution differences, not model design, are the primary constraint. Zero-shot results reinforce this: even the best transfer direction achieves only 34.68%. FD(C-BESD vs FAU)=8.50 vs FD(C-BESD vs IEMOCAP)=7.20—speaking style creates greater divergence than speaker age.

### 5.2 Architectural Choices Are Secondary

Across B1, B4, and B6, architectural variations affect performance within ±2–5pp compared to the ~25pp corpus gap. The Adapter module is neutral to harmful in all configurations. Layer fusion contributes negligibly: any layer L≥7 performs equivalently to learned weighted fusion.

### 5.3 Transfer Learning: Target Domain Asymmetry

C-BESD as a target achieves 91%+ from any source—an unexpected finding. This may reflect C-BESD's high-quality acted recordings with clear inter-class boundaries. When the target provides a clean learning signal, source quality matters less.

### 5.4 Per-Class Analysis

Class-level difficulty is corpus-dependent, not universal. *Sad* is unrecognizable on FAU but well-separated on C-BESD. This has implications for real-world deployment where class distributions differ from training.

### 5.5 Implications

Future progress in children's SER depends less on novel architectures and more on addressing distribution shift: (1) larger spontaneous child speech corpora; (2) domain adaptation targeting spontaneous child speech; (3) augmentation strategies matching the target child speech distribution.

### 5.6 Limitations

WavLM-based only; three corpora; 3-seed design insufficient for formal statistical testing; 24 of 27 B1 checkpoints not preserved.

---

## 6. Conclusion

We presented a systematic 192-experiment ablation study diagnosing the factors limiting children's speech emotion recognition. Our central finding is that distribution shift between corpora—not architectural design choices—is the dominant bottleneck. The 25pp acted-spontaneous gap persists across all architectural configurations. Zero-shot cross-corpus transfer collapses to 19–35%, and transfer success is determined primarily by the target domain. Self-attention pooling provides consistent gains while adapter modules and layer fusion contribute negligibly.

These results indicate that future advances in children's SER require addressing the acted-spontaneous distribution gap through better data, domain adaptation, and augmentation strategies, rather than through incremental architectural refinements.
